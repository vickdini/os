During the last couple of decades, a large number of cloud-edge tools has been created, though not systematized. In fact, installing and using them can result cumbersome to the general user and require sophisticated technical knowledge. Moreover, computers have been historically limited by the hardware available. This will all change with Hydra, a UNIX-like multi-cloud operating system, which will abstract from the users the ordeal of managing resources in multiple clouds and take advantage of fog-edge computing. Hydra will be a micro-kernel-based operating system with Wayland, which will work with most open-source desktop environments, such as GNOME and KDE. In addition to these characteristics, the fact that Hydra is UNIX-like will ease the transition from current UNIX-based operating systems. Another highly relevant feature will be the compiler for this OS: it will compile programs that will run on the system without the programmer, nor the user, having to worry about the intricacies of optimal multi-cloud, fog, and edge resource management (optimality in this case doesn’t refer only to the technological features, but also to their respective economics). Hydra’s implementation of self-* properties will also endure its resilience and autonomous evolution in an ever-changing world that constantly undergoes cracker attacks and ecosystem failures. Not only CPUs will be considered, but also GPGPUs, TPUs, FPGAs, and SoCs, thereby making the most of the computational resources locally and remotely available. Current technologies and those that will be released in the foreseeable future make Hydra possible (e.g. higher connection speeds, lower latency, de-centralized self-* software architectures, resource sharing strategies, economies of scale benefits, and vendor agnosticism). Hydra will also benefit the environment, as energy consumption will decrease significantly by moving computational resources to centralized locations that will also favor the use of environmentally-friendly power sources and by increasing the mean time between computer replacement, as users won’t have to continually replace parts with more powerful ones.
